User-Triggered Agents}

\begin{abstract}
Studies of AI crawler blocking compare an AI user agent's page to a browser's, yet such a
request claims an identity it cannot prove. We request the Tranco top \R{design.headList} and a
sample of smaller sites with \R{design.ai} AI agents, neutral controls and spoofed Googlebot and
Bingbot, from a residential and a datacenter vantage point. Robots.txt rules and server refusals
mostly fall on different sites. Beyond the rate at which sites refuse a spoofed search engine,
only training crawlers are refused more often. Where a spoofed user-triggered agent was refused,
the real ChatGPT failed on \pct{val2.ChatGPT.blocked} of checks; Claude was mostly stopped by its
own fetch tool. HTTP~402 to AI agents carries no price.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Website owners are asked to decide whether AI companies may collect their pages. The standard
instrument is robots.txt \citep{rfc9309}: a site names crawler tokens such as \texttt{GPTBot}
and the paths they should not fetch. Many studies count these rules
\citep{longpre2024consent,fletcher2024news,steinacker2026misinfo}. A robots.txt file is a request,
though; whom a site actually serves is decided by its server, its content-delivery network (CDN)
and its bot management. Recent work therefore also requests pages with AI crawler user agents and
compares the answer with a browser's \citep{liu2025somesite,steinacker2026misinfo,hasdata2026index}.

That comparison mixes three causes. A site may refuse anything that looks like a bot; it may
refuse a request that \emph{claims} a known crawler identity but does not come from that
crawler's network; or it may refuse AI agents as such. We separate them. Every homepage is
requested with a browser user agent, \R{design.ai} AI agents of five operators in three roles
(training, search, user-triggered), spoofed Googlebot and Bingbot, and two neutral controls. A
refusal is \emph{AI-specific} only if both controls are served. Spoofed search engines give the
baseline: no site means to keep out the real Googlebot, so refusing its name from our address
measures identity verification. We find:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Declared and enforced blocks mostly fall on different sites.} Of the sites that
        block an AI agent in robots.txt or AI-specifically on the server, only
        \rng{w1head.de.x.crossAiBothShare} (per agent) do both among top sites.
  \item \textbf{Only training crawlers exceed the verification baseline.} On sites that serve
        both spoofed search engines, training crawlers are still refused on
        \rng{w1head.de.x.beyondRef.training} of top sites, search and user-triggered agents on
        \rng{w1head.de.x.beyondRef.search} and \rng{w1head.de.x.beyondRef.user}. Paired
        tests against spoofed Googlebot reject equality for all training crawlers and for no
        search or user-triggered agent in the excess direction.
  \item \textbf{Spoofed refusals predict real ones only in part.} Where a site refused the spoofed
        ChatGPT-User, the real ChatGPT also failed in \R{val2.ChatGPT.blocked.k} of
        \R{val2.ChatGPT.blocked.n} checks, against \R{val2.ChatGPT.served.k} of
        \R{val2.ChatGPT.served.n} where it was served. \R{val2.Claude.blocked.tool} of the
        \R{val2.Claude.blocked.k} refusals the real Claude met came from its own fetch tool, not
        from the site.
  \item \textbf{HTTP~402 is AI-specific but not a price.} It reaches AI agents on
        \ci{w1head.de.any.payment} of top sites; the controls are served on
        \ci{w1head.de.x.pay.controlsServed} of them, but only \R{w1head.de.pay.priceHeader.k}
        sends a price, which pay-per-crawl gives only to registered, signing crawlers
        \citep{cloudflare2025paypercrawl}.
\end{itemize}
The study was pre-registered; analyses added later are marked exploratory.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{Declared policy.} \citet{longpre2024consent} audit robots.txt and terms of service
behind large AI training corpora and find AI restrictions rising quickly;
\citet{fletcher2024news} counts AI blocks in robots.txt of leading news sites.
\citet{kim2025scrapers} observe in server logs that bots follow robots.txt selectively, least for
strict rules.

\paragraph{Enforced policy.} \citet{liu2025somesite} request the top 10k sites with a headless
browser and with two Anthropic user agents, chosen because Anthropic published no IP ranges that
sites could verify; they find active blocking common and rarely backed by robots.txt, and
examine Cloudflare's AI-bot blocking. \citet{steinacker2026misinfo} compare robots.txt rules and
user-agent refusals between reputable news and misinformation sites. An industry report (not
peer reviewed) tests GPTBot against a browser on about two thousand sites
\citep{hasdata2026index}. These studies compare one AI user agent with a browser. We add
neutral and search-engine baselines that separate identity verification from AI policy, three
roles per operator, a random sample of smaller sites, two vantage points, and checks with the
real agents of the same role.

\paragraph{Real agents and cloaking.} \citet{seiden2026canary} serve canary tokens to scrapers
and ask LLMs about them to learn which scraper feeds which model; we use canary pages only to show
that an agent fetches live. Serving crawlers a different page is known from search spam;
\citet{invernizzi2016cloak} detect it by comparing fetches under several browsing profiles.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
The protocol was frozen before the main run (\texttt{PROTOCOL.md}, commit \texttt{486ec1f}, archived with the registration) and
registered on OSF the same day
\ifanon(link withheld)\else(\url{https://osf.io/prwng}, \osfdoi)\fi. Analyses not in it are
marked \emph{exploratory}; every change is in its deviation log (Section~\ref{sec:deviations}).

\paragraph{Population.} Tranco list GQ3LK \citep{lepochat2019tranco}, in two strata reported
separately: the \emph{head}, ranks 1 to \R{design.headList}, and the \emph{tail},
\R{design.tailList} domains drawn uniformly at random from the ranks below. A domain is measured
only if its robots.txt is reachable, since RFC~9309 reads an unreachable file as ``disallow
all'', and allows our study token. In the head, \R{w1head.de.funnel.robotsUnreachable} domains
failed the first test; \R{w1head.de.x.unreach.dns} of them have no DNS entry,
\R{w1head.de.x.unreach.connect} refused or timed out the connection,
\R{w1head.de.x.unreach.tls} failed TLS and \R{w1head.de.x.unreach.5xx} answered with a 5xx
status; only the last and some of the timeouts can be refusals of our requests. \R{w1head.x.vf.both} were unreachable
from both vantage points. \R{w1head.de.funnel.robotsDisallowStudy} disallowed our token,
\R{w1head.de.x.disallow.all} of them by blocking every crawler name we use;
\R{w1head.de.funnel.noWebsite} served no HTML. Of \R{w1head.de.funnel.probed} probed domains,
\R{w1head.de.funnel.comparable} were \emph{comparable}: both browser-UA fetches returned the
same readable page. In the tail, \R{w1tail.de.funnel.comparable} of
\R{w1tail.de.funnel.probed} probed domains were comparable.

\paragraph{Agents.} Each homepage is requested with \R{design.arms} user agents: two identical
requests with a desktop Chrome user agent (the \emph{browser-UA} baseline), Googlebot and
Bingbot, \R{design.ai} AI agents with the user-agent strings their operators publish, and two
controls in the same crawler-style shape whose product tokens are
\texttt{\R{design.ua.control-plain.product}} and \texttt{\R{design.ua.control-bot.product}}. A
rule against ``bot'' in the user agent refuses the second control, so such sites never count as
AI-specific. Roles follow the operators' descriptions: training (GPTBot, ClaudeBot,
Meta-ExternalAgent, CCBot), search (OAI-SearchBot, Claude-SearchBot, PerplexityBot) and
user-triggered (ChatGPT-User, Claude-User, Perplexity-User).

\paragraph{Procedure.} We fetch robots.txt with a study user agent that links to an information
and opt-out page, then the homepage with the browser user agent. Every other agent then requests
its final URL once, in random order, \R{design.gapMs}\,ms apart, without cookies or JavaScript,
with no retries. All arms share one HTTP client, and thus one TLS fingerprint.

\paragraph{Classification.} A response is \emph{blocked} on a 4xx or 5xx status (except 402 and
429), a bot-management challenge or an empty 202, and only if a later request to the same site
still got the page, so that a site that stops answering us is not counted. Content
\emph{differs} when the Jaccard similarity of word 3-shingles to the browser-UA page is below
\R{design.same} and at least \R{design.margin} below that of the two browser-UA fetches.
Declared policy is a full block of the agent's token in robots.txt under RFC~9309 matching. A
block is \emph{AI-specific} when both controls got the browser-UA page. The unit is the
comparable domain; brackets give Wilson 95\,\% intervals.

\paragraph{Baseline test (exploratory).} Per AI agent we compare its AI-specific blocks with
spoofed Googlebot's on the same sites (exact McNemar test, Holm-adjusted over the
\R{design.ai} agents), and count AI-specific blocks on sites that serve both spoofed search
engines, which identity verification cannot explain.

\paragraph{Vantage points and waves.} The residential vantage point is a consumer line in Germany
(AS3320), the datacenter one a GitHub-hosted runner in Microsoft Azure. Wave~1 measured both
strata from both within two days. Wave~2 measured again every domain with a block, a 402 or a
content difference in wave~1 (\R{design.wave2.head} head and \R{design.wave2.tail} tail
domains), one day later.

\paragraph{Real agents.} Two rounds, each through the author's consumer accounts, in a fresh
chat per check, asking the real ChatGPT, Claude or Perplexity to open a homepage and state its
main heading and topic; answers are coded as content, refused/failed or unclear against the
browser-UA page. These agents fetch from their operators' networks. The pre-registered round
drew head sites with an AI-specific block for GPTBot, ClaudeBot or PerplexityBot. The second
(exploratory) round draws, per operator, \R{design.val2.blocked} sites where its spoofed
user-triggered agent had an AI-specific block and \R{design.val2.served} where it was served;
repeats every ``content'' answer in a fresh chat that forbids browsing, coding it unclear if the
model names the heading from memory; and first opens a canary page with a random phrase that
exists nowhere else, to show that the agent fetches live. An agent that fails its canary is not
tested further. Refusals are split into those by the agent's own fetch tool (``site blocked'',
robots rules) and those by the site; an answer based on an outdated cached copy is unclear.

\paragraph{Real browser (exploratory).} To test the browser-UA baseline, \R{bc.comparable.n}
comparable and \R{bc.notComparable.n} not comparable head sites, drawn at random, were opened
once in Microsoft Edge (Playwright, headless) from the residential line.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
Unless stated otherwise: head stratum, wave~1, residential vantage point.

\subsection{Declared versus enforced}
Robots.txt fully blocks at least one AI agent on \ci{w1head.de.any.declaredFullBlock} of top
sites. The server refuses at least one AI agent while serving both controls on
\ci{w1head.de.any.aiSpecificBlock}; on \ci{w1head.de.any.enforcedDespiteAllowed} the refused agent
is allowed by robots.txt. For GPTBot, \R{w1head.de.x.crossAi.gptbot.declaredAndEnforced} sites
declare and AI-specifically enforce a block, \R{w1head.de.x.crossAi.gptbot.declaredOnly} only
declare it and \R{w1head.de.x.crossAi.gptbot.enforcedOnly} only enforce it
(Table~\ref{tab:cross}). Among smaller sites the overlap is
\rng{w1tail.de.x.crossAiBothShare}; they declare less (\ci{w1tail.de.any.declaredFullBlock})
and enforce more (\ci{w1tail.de.any.aiSpecificBlock}). Tokens we did not request with are
declared at similar rates: Google-Extended on \ci{w1head.de.x.tok.Google-Extended},

\subsection{Who is refused}
\label{sec:who}
Spoofed Googlebot and Bingbot are almost never blocked in robots.txt
(\pct{w1head.de.arm.googlebot.declaredFullBlock} and \pct{w1head.de.arm.bingbot.declaredFullBlock})
but refused AI-specifically on \ci{w1head.de.arm.googlebot.aiSpecificBlock} and
\ci{w1head.de.arm.bingbot.aiSpecificBlock} of top sites: these sites serve two unknown crawlers
yet refuse a known name sent from outside its network. Search and user-triggered AI agents are
refused at about this rate (\rng{w1head.de.roleAiSpecificBlock.search} and
\rng{w1head.de.roleAiSpecificBlock.user} per agent), training crawlers more often (\rng{w1head.de.roleAiSpecificBlock.training}).

The overlap per site shows that this is not a coincidence of rates (Figure~\ref{fig:arms}).
\rng{w1head.de.x.alsoRef.search} of the AI-specific blocks of search agents and
\rng{w1head.de.x.alsoRef.user} of those of user-triggered agents fall on sites that also refuse
a spoofed search engine, against \rng{w1head.de.x.alsoRef.training} for training crawlers. On
sites that serve both search engines, training crawlers are still refused on
\rng{w1head.de.x.beyondRef.training}, search and user-triggered agents on only
\rng{w1head.de.x.beyondRef.search} and \rng{w1head.de.x.beyondRef.user}. Paired against spoofed
Googlebot, all \R{w1head.de.x.moreThanGb.training} training crawlers are refused more often
(\R{w1head.de.x.pHolmMax.training}, Holm); no search or user-triggered agent is, and
\R{w1head.de.x.lessThanGb.search} search and \R{w1head.de.x.lessThanGb.user} user-triggered
agent are refused significantly \emph{less} often. The same holds among smaller sites (beyond
the baseline: training \rng{w1tail.de.x.beyondRef.training}, search
\rng{w1tail.de.x.beyondRef.search}, user-triggered \rng{w1tail.de.x.beyondRef.user}) and from
the datacenter (training \rng{w1head.az.x.beyondRef.training}, search
\rng{w1head.az.x.beyondRef.search}). Most sites that refuse search and user-triggered agents
thus appear to check identities, not to target AI; only training crawlers meet a rule of their
own.

\subsection{Real agents}
\label{sec:validation}
In the pre-registered round the real user-triggered agent was refused or failed on
\R{val.ChatGPT.refused-failed} of \R{val.ChatGPT.alsoBlocked.n} decided checks for ChatGPT,
\R{val.Claude.refused-failed} of \R{val.Claude.alsoBlocked.n} for Claude and
\R{val.Perplexity.refused-failed} of \R{val.Perplexity.alsoBlocked.n} for Perplexity. Those sites
had refused the operator's \emph{crawler}, so the round mixes verification with role. Split by what
the spoofed user-triggered agent of the same operator met on the same site, the real ChatGPT was
refused on \ci{val.ChatGPT.same.ai-specific-block} of sites that refused its spoofed twin and on
\ci{val.ChatGPT.same.served} of sites that served it; for Claude the shares are
\ci{val.Claude.same.ai-specific-block} and \ci{val.Claude.same.served}.

The second round tests this directly. ChatGPT and Claude read their canary pages live;
Perplexity (free plan) failed its canary twice, so its round was not run: its answers could not
show live access. Where the spoofed ChatGPT-User had been refused, the real ChatGPT failed on
\ci{val2.ChatGPT.blocked} of decided checks; where it had been served, on
\ci{val2.ChatGPT.served}. For Claude the shares are \ci{val2.Claude.blocked} and
\ci{val2.Claude.served}, but \R{val2.Claude.blocked.tool} of the \R{val2.Claude.blocked.k}
refusals came from Claude's own fetch tool, which declined the site before fetching,
\R{val2.Claude.blocked.toolRobots} of them on sites whose robots.txt fully disallows Claude-User;
\R{val2.Claude.blocked.site} were refusals by the site. Another \R{val2.Claude.blocked.stale}
answers rested on an outdated cached copy. The memory control flagged
\R{val2.ChatGPT.blocked.memKnows} of \R{val2.ChatGPT.blocked.memChecks} ChatGPT and
\R{val2.Claude.blocked.memKnows} of \R{val2.Claude.blocked.memChecks} Claude content answers
on these sites. A spoofed refusal thus predicts a real one for ChatGPT on about half of the
sites, while the real agent rarely fails where the spoof was served; for Claude, what the real agent meets is
largely its operator's own robots.txt compliance, which a spoofed measurement cannot observe.
Spoofed shares describe how sites treat traffic that \emph{claims} to be an AI agent.

\subsection{Robustness}
\label{sec:robust}
\emph{Browser baseline.} On comparable sites the real browser got a readable page on
\ci{bc.comparable.realReadable} and the same title as our browser-UA fetch on
\ci{bc.comparable.sameTitle}; a headless browser is itself refused by some bot management, so it
is no gold standard. On not comparable sites our client was refused on
\ci{bc.notComparable.oursRefused}, but the real browser got a readable page where ours was
refused on only \ci{bc.notComparable.oursRefusedRealReadable}: few sites drop out because they
single out our client; most refuse automated clients of any kind.

\emph{Order.} Refusals of any kind are as frequent early in the random order
(\ci{w1head.de.x.order.rawEarly}) as late (\ci{w1head.de.x.order.rawLate}), so the burst of
requests does not itself trigger blocking. \ci{w1head.de.x.order.unconfirmedShare} of refusals of
AI agents had no later served request and are not counted; since the order is random per site,
this lowers all shares alike.

\emph{Who refuses (exploratory).} Of AI-specific blocks of AI agents,
\ci{w1head.de.x.layer.ai.signature} carry a bot-management signature (challenge page,
\texttt{cf-mitigated}, CDN block page), \ci{w1head.de.x.layer.ai.cdn-header-only} pass a CDN
without one and \ci{w1head.de.x.layer.ai.none} show neither; CDN headers alone do not show that
the CDN refused.

\emph{Vantage point and time.} From the datacenter, the share with an AI-specific block is
\ci{w1head.az.any.aiSpecificBlock}; per-agent agreement between vantage points is
$\kappa=\R{w1head.cv.kappaAi.min}$--$\R{w1head.cv.kappaAi.max}$ in the head and
$\R{w1tail.cv.kappaAi.min}$--$\R{w1tail.cv.kappaAi.max}$ in the tail. Wave~2, one day later,
shows repeatability, not stability over time: almost all AI-specific blocks recur
(Table~\ref{tab:repeat}).

\subsection{Pay-per-crawl}
At least one AI agent received HTTP~402 on \ci{w1head.de.any.payment} of top sites
(\R{w1head.de.pay.domains} sites) and \ci{w1tail.de.any.payment} of smaller sites. On these top
sites the controls were served on \ci{w1head.de.x.pay.controlsServed} and a spoofed search engine
got a 402 on \ci{w1head.de.x.pay.searchEngine402}: the 402 targets AI agents. Only
\R{w1head.de.pay.priceHeader.k} sent the \texttt{crawler-price} header. This is expected and
says little about a market: pay-per-crawl quotes prices only to crawlers registered with the CDN
that sign their requests \citep{cloudflare2025paypercrawl}, which a spoofed request cannot do.

\subsection{Different content}
\label{sec:rq2}
An AI agent got substantially different content while both controls got the browser-UA page on
\ci{w1head.de.rq2.anyAiDiff} of top sites, the same share as for Googlebot or Bingbot
(\ci{w1head.de.rq2.anySearchEngineDiff}). The planned manual coding was not done
(Section~\ref{sec:deviations}), so these are upper bounds and we claim no AI-specific content.

\section{Ethics}
\label{sec:ethics}
The study has no human subjects; we followed the principles of the Menlo Report
\citep{menlo2012}. As an independent researcher the author had no institutional review board;
the design was published on OSF before the main run.

\emph{Load.} One request per agent and homepage, \R{design.gapMs}\,ms apart, homepages only,
no retries after an HTTP answer.

\emph{robots.txt.} We obeyed it for our study token and did not contact sites further whose
robots.txt was unreachable. The agent requests, however, reached sites whose robots.txt
disallows those agents' tokens. We judged this acceptable because the rules address the
operators' crawlers, each such site received one homepage request per token, the request is the
minimal observation of enforcement and the established method of prior work
\citep{liu2025somesite,steinacker2026misinfo}, and no content beyond page features is kept or
released.

\emph{Spoofed identities.} Our requests carry the names of Google, Microsoft and five AI
operators. A site that verifies identities classes them as fake; one that does not may log
about a dozen requests from our address as these operators' traffic. We consider this harm
small and disclose it here and on the information page. The opt-out link was in the study user
agent only, which fetched robots.txt; the agent requests carried the operators' strings.
Opt-outs received: \R{w1head.de.funnel.optedOut}.

\emph{Real agents.} The checks ran in the author's own consumer accounts, typed in sequence by a
browser assistant under the author's supervision. Automated use of consumer
interfaces may conflict with operators' terms; we kept it to the checks reported. Adult sites
were skipped.

\emph{Release.} No site is named. The per-site file replaces each domain with a keyed hash and its
rank with a rank band; this stops casual naming, not determined re-identification.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
\textbf{Spoofed user agents} come from our two addresses only; the real-agent checks are small
and through personal accounts. \textbf{Homepages only, no JavaScript;} sites that refuse our
client drop out as not comparable (Section~\ref{sec:robust}). \textbf{The robots.txt gate}
excludes sites that disallow our token; they declare more AI blocks
(\R{w1head.de.x.disallow.gptbot} of \R{w1head.de.x.disallow.n} block GPTBot), so declared shares
are lower bounds. \textbf{Content differences} are uncoded. \textbf{Time:} two waves one day
apart, one residential line in one country. \textbf{Exploratory} analyses were chosen after
seeing the pre-registered results.

\section{Deviations from the Protocol}
\label{sec:deviations}
(i)~Residential runs restarted automatically after native crashes of the runtime; domains in
flight were measured again from scratch. (ii)~An analysis bug passed the array index as threshold,
so all but the first run were analysed without robots.txt parsing; it was found and fixed on the
first analysis, before any result was reported. (iii)~The real-agent prompt asked for the main
heading and topic instead of a verbatim quote, which one agent declined. (iv)~The manual coding
of content differences was not done. (v)~Wave~2 ran one day after wave~1 instead of at least
seven days later, and no later wave followed. (vi)~The baseline test, the real-browser check, the
second real-agent round, the signature attribution and the order analysis were added after an
internal pre-review. (vii)~In that round Perplexity failed its canary and was not tested, and
adult sites were skipped.

\section{Conclusion}
What robots.txt says and what servers do are two policies that rarely coincide on the same site.
Measuring the second by spoofing user agents mixes AI policy with verification of crawler
identity. With spoofed search engines as a baseline, only training crawlers meet AI-specific
rules; the refusals that search and user-triggered agents meet are mostly identity checks.
Real agents bring a third party into view: the operator's own fetch tool, which may decline a
site because of its robots.txt before the site can decide. Studies of AI access should report such
baselines, test real agents of the same role, and not read a 402 to an unverified request as a
price.
